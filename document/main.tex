\subsection{Offset-Free MPC}
In this subsection, we will present a standard offset free model predictive control framework, introduced by~\cite{pannocchia2003disturbance}. This approach consist of state estimation - observer, target optimization and dynamic optimization. 
The whole approach is used to introduce integral action into model predictive control to help mitigate plant-model missmatch and ensure offset free control. If this compensation would not be used, the plant with this controller would not reach a desired reference (other than at origin), due to predictions using different model than actual plant. 

The offset-free augmented system can be written in the following state-space form, including the disturbance input matrix $B_d$ and disturbance output matrix $C_d$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:of:ss}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    x_{k+1} \\
    d_{k+1}
  \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    A & B_d \\
    0 & I
  \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    x_k \\
    d_k
  \end{bmatrix}
  +
  \begin{bmatrix}
    B \\
    0
  \end{bmatrix}
  u_k,
  \qquad
  y_k =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    C & C_d
  \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    x_k \\
    d_k
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $x_k$ is the original system state, $d_k$ is the estimated constant disturbance, $u_k$ is the control input, $y_k$ is the output, $A$, $B$, and $C$ are the original state-space matrices, $B_d$ and $C_d$ encode how the disturbance enters the state and output equations, and $I$ is the identity matrix.
Here, $n_x$, $n_u$, $n_y$, and $n_d$ denote the state, input, output, and disturbance dimensions, respectively, so that $x_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x}$, $u_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u}$, $y_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$, $d_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_d}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x \times n_x}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x \times n_u}$, $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_x}$, $B_d \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x \times n_d}$, $C_d \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_d}$, and $I \in \mathbb{R}^{n_d \times n_d}$.
For the offset free behavior, the number of the disturbances $n_d$ must be lower or equal to the number of the measurements $n_y$. Also it is necessary to note that the number of offset free tracking is achievable only in the number of disturbances that are estimated. For the system where $n_d = n_y$ system and choice of the matrices $B_d$ and $C_d$ there is a necessary condition, that:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:of:rank}
  \operatorname{rank}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    I - A & -B_d \\
    C & C_d
  \end{bmatrix}
  = n_x + n_y.
\end{equation}

When solving the MPC, both states and disturbance are approximated by observer.

\label{sec:easy-mpc}

In this subsection, we will present a standard offset free model predictive control framework using approximation of Koopman operator as a linear time invariant (LTI) model. As observer is standard Kalman filter, we will discuss mainly remaining two optimization problems. 
As we use the LTI approximation of Koopman operator, we can use the standard MPC formulation with linear constraints and objective function. The control structure is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:close_loop}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/close_loop.pdf}

  \label{fig:close_loop}
\end{figure}

The target optimization problem computes the steady-state targets $\bar{u}, \bar{z}, \bar{y}$ that minimizes the difference between reference $y_\text{ref}$ and feasible output $\bar{y}$ in steady state. The problem is formulated as: 

\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:target:opt}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{\bar{u}, \bar{z}, \bar{y}} \quad & \; (\bar{y} - y_\text{ref})^\intercal \ui{Q}{y}^\text{t} (\bar{y} - y_\text{ref}) \label{eq:target:opt:obj}, \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \; \bar{z} = A\bar{z} + B\bar{u} \label{eq:target:opt:z}, \\
    & \; \bar{y} = C\bar{z} + d \label{eq:target:opt:y}, \\
    & \; u_{\min} \leq \bar{u} \leq u_{\max} \label{eq:target:opt:u}, \\
    & \; y_{\min} \leq \bar{y} \leq y_{\max} \label{eq:target:opt:ycon}, \\
    & \; d = \hat{d}(t), \quad y_\text{ref} = y_\text{sp}(t), \label{eq:target:opt:params}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\bar{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u}$, $\bar{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z}$, and $\bar{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ are the steady-state input, lifted state, and output, respectively; $\ui{Q}{y}^\text{t} \succ 0$ is the output weight of the target optimization, which is tuned separately from the weights of the MPC problem; $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z \times n_z}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z \times n_u}$, $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_z}$ are the state-space matrices of the LTI Koopman model; $d = \hat{d}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ is the additive output disturbance estimate provided by the Kalman filter; $y_\text{ref} = y_\text{sp}(t)$ is the output setpoint; and $u_{\min}, u_{\max}$, $y_{\min}, y_{\max}$ are the input and output constraint bounds.

Given the steady-state target $(\bar{u}, \bar{z}, \bar{y})$, the MPC problem is solved at each sampling instant to compute the optimal input sequence over the horizon $N$:

\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:of:mpc}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{u_0, \ldots, u_{N-1}} \; & \; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left[ (y_k - \bar{y})^\intercal Q_\text{y} (y_k - \bar{y}) + \Delta u_k^\intercal Q_{\Delta\text{u}} \Delta u_k + (u_k - \bar{u})^\intercal Q_\text{u} (u_k - \bar{u}) \right], \label{eq:of:mpc:obj} \\
    \text{s.t.} \;\;\;\; & \; z_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:z} \\
    & \; y_k = C z_k + d, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:y} \\
    & \; \Delta u_k = u_k - u_{k-1}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:du} \\
    & \; u_{\min} \leq u_k \leq u_{\max}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:u} \\
    & \; y_{\min} \leq y_k \leq y_{\max}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:ycon} \\
    & \; z_0 = \hat{z}(t), \quad d = \hat{d}(t), \quad u_{-1} = u(t-T_\text{s}), \label{eq:of:mpc:init}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $Q_\text{y} \succ 0$ is the output tracking weight matrix, $Q_{\Delta\text{u}} \succ 0$ the input increment weight matrix, and $Q_\text{u} \succeq 0$ the weight on the deviation of the input from its steady-state target; $\hat{z}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z}$ is the lifted state estimate provided by the Kalman filter; and $u_{-1} = u(t - T_\text{s})$ is the input applied at the previous sampling instant, used to compute the first increment $\Delta u_0$. The first term of the objective function~\eqref{eq:of:mpc:obj} penalizes the predicted tracking error with respect to the output target, the second term penalizes the movement of the manipulated variables, and the third term keeps the inputs at the steady-state target computed by~\eqref{eq:target:opt}, which resolves the remaining degrees of freedom when the plant has more inputs than outputs. Following the receding-horizon principle, only the first element $u_0^*$ of the optimal sequence is applied to the plant.



\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

In this section, we present the proposed offset-free Koopman MPC framework. The main drawback of the current Koopman MPC formulations, whether offset-free, robust, or of any other kind, is that the optimization is carried out without fully exploiting the knowledge of the lifted space.
The prediction is made in the lifted space, but the objective function is expressed in the original space, which is recovered from the lifted state by the linear transformation \(y_k = C z_k\). This transformation is nonlinear by nature, \(y_k = h(z_k)\), and it is restricted to a linear one only so that a linear MPC problem is obtained. Formulations that do not use this transformation directly usually rely on an equivalent one, for example by augmenting the lifted state with the measurements, in which case the recovery of the measurements from the lifted state is again linear.

We show that the lifted space can be used directly in the objective function, and we derive the corresponding transformation of the weighting matrix.
The back transformation is thereby eliminated from the objective function of the MPC problem, while it is still required to compute the steady-state target and to impose the output constraints. In both of these, we approximate it by a first-order Taylor expansion.

Both prediction models used in this work are obtained with the deep Koopman architecture of Fig.~\ref{fig:DK-nonautonomous}, in which the encoder lifts the measured outputs, the lifted state evolves linearly, and the decoder projects the lifted state back to the outputs. Given an input--output trajectory of the plant, the model is unrolled over a window of $N_\text{r}$ samples as
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:dk:rollout}
  \begin{align}
    z_0 &= g(y_0), \label{eq:dk:rollout:init} \\
    z_{i+1} &= A z_i + B u_i, \quad i\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N_\text{r}-1}, \label{eq:dk:rollout:dyn} \\
    \hat{y}_i &= h(z_i), \quad i\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N_\text{r}}, \label{eq:dk:rollout:out}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $N_\text{r}$ is the length of the training window, $i$ is the sample index within this window, $y_i$ and $u_i$ are the measured output and the applied input taken from the data, $z_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z}$ is the lifted state, and $\hat{y}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ is the predicted output. The encoder $g$ and the decoder $h$ are multilayer perceptrons, while the input map $B$ is kept linear, so that the identified model is an LTI system in the lifted coordinates and can be used directly in the optimization problems~\eqref{eq:cs:target} and~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc}.

The four terms of the training loss are the multi-step output prediction error over the whole window, the consistency between the unrolled lifted state and the lifted measurement, the one-step output prediction error, and the reconstruction error of the autoencoder, resulting in
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dk:loss}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{L} = {} & \frac{\ui{w}{y}}{N_\text{r}-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N_\text{r}-1} \left\| \hat{y}_i - y_i \right\|_2^2
    + \frac{\ui{w}{z}}{N_\text{r}-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N_\text{r}-1} \left\| z_i - g(y_i) - B u_i \right\|_2^2 \\
    & + w_1 \left\| \hat{y}_1 - y_1 \right\|_2^2
    + \ui{w}{r} \left\| \hat{y}_0 - y_0 \right\|_2^2,
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ is the loss minimized during training and $\ui{w}{y}$, $\ui{w}{z}$, $w_1$, and $\ui{w}{r}$ are the weights of the four terms, respectively. All terms are additionally averaged over the minibatch of training windows. The one-step term $w_1$ is fixed to one, so that only three weights have to be chosen, and the remaining weights are scaled relative to it.

When the decoder is restricted to a single matrix, $h(z) = Cz$, the identified model is the LTI model of the standard offset-free Koopman MPC of Sec.~\ref{sec:easy-mpc}. When the decoder is a nonlinear map, the model is the one used by the proposed scheme, and a constant output matrix is obtained afterwards by linear least squares from the lifted trajectory of the training data. This matrix is not used in the objective function or in the constraints of~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc}; it only provides the initial output matrix of the observer of Sec.~\ref{sec:tvkf}, which is replaced by the Jacobian $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ at every subsequent sampling instant.

The lifted dimension $n_z$, the depth and the width of the encoder and the decoder, the activation function, the weights in~\eqref{eq:dk:loss}, the window length $N_\text{r}$, the minibatch size, and the learning rate are not tuned by hand. Each candidate configuration is trained, the resulting model is inserted into the closed loop, and the achieved value of the closed-loop objective function is used as the score of the configuration; the search itself is carried out with the tree-structured Parzen estimator of Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna}. Scoring a model by the closed-loop performance rather than by the open-loop prediction error is a deliberate choice, because the two are only loosely related, as the results of Sec.~\ref{sec:ident} and Sec.~\ref{sec:Results} show. The same criterion is used to select the order and the horizons of the subspace model that serves as the baseline, so that no model is favored by the way its parameters were chosen. Note that this procedure is not a contribution of this work and any comparable model selection scheme can replace it.

\subsection{Linearization of the output mapping}
For the control problem, the transformation from the lifted space back to the original space cannot be eliminated completely, because both the steady-state target and the output constraints are expressed in the original variables. We therefore approximate the output mapping by a first-order Taylor expansion, and we consider two linearization points, the target computed at the previous sampling instant and the current state estimate, which gives
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:linearization}
  \begin{align}
    y_k &= h(z_k) \approx h\!\left(\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})\right) + H\!\left(\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})\right)\!\left(z_k - \bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})\right), \label{eq:linearization:target} \\
    y_k &= h(z_k) \approx h\!\left(\hat{z}(t)\right) + H\!\left(\hat{z}(t)\right)\!\left(z_k - \hat{z}(t)\right), \label{eq:linearization:state}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $h : \mathbb{R}^{n_z} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ is the decoder of the identified model, which acts as the nonlinear output mapping; $H(\cdot) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_z}$ is the Jacobian of $h$ evaluated at its argument; and $\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})$ is the steady-state lifted state computed by the target optimizer at the previous sampling instant.

\subsection{Target optimization}

The objective function of the target optimization is the same as in the standard offset-free MPC of Sec.~\ref{sec:easy-mpc}. The difference is in the output equation, where the linear map of~\eqref{eq:target:opt:y} is replaced by the linearization~\eqref{eq:linearization:target} of the nonlinear output mapping. The proposed target optimization problem is therefore formulated as:

\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:cs:target}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{\bar{u}, \bar{z}, \bar{y}} \quad & \; (\bar{y} - y_\text{ref})^\intercal \ui{Q}{y}^\text{t} (\bar{y} - y_\text{ref}), \label{eq:cs:target:obj} \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \; \bar{z} = A\bar{z} + B\bar{u}, \label{eq:cs:target:z} \\
    & \; \bar{y} = H(\ui{z}{LP})\bar{z} + h(\ui{z}{LP}) - H(\ui{z}{LP})\ui{z}{LP} + d, \label{eq:cs:target:y} \\
    & \; u_{\min} \leq \bar{u} \leq u_{\max}, \label{eq:cs:target:u} \\
    & \; y_{\min} \leq \bar{y} \leq y_{\max}, \label{eq:cs:target:ycon} \\
    & \; d = \hat{d}(t), \quad y_\text{ref} = y_\text{sp}(t), \quad \ui{z}{LP} = \bar{z}(t-T_\text{s}), \label{eq:cs:target:params}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where \(\ui{z}{LP}\) is the linearization point at which the output mapping is approximated by its first-order Taylor expansion. We propose to choose this point as close as possible to \(\bar{z}\), and we therefore use the target \(\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})\) computed at the previous sampling instant, which corresponds to the linearization~\eqref{eq:linearization:target}. This choice improves the accuracy of the approximation of the transformation from the lifted space to the original space and, consequently, the accuracy of the computed target.


\subsection{MPC}
The proposed MPC problem is formulated as:
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:cs:mpc}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{u_0, \ldots, u_{N-1}} \; & \; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left[ (z_k - \bar{z})^\intercal Q_\text{z} (z_k - \bar{z}) + \Delta u_k^\intercal Q_{\Delta\text{u}} \Delta u_k + (u_k - \bar{u})^\intercal Q_\text{u} (u_k - \bar{u}) \right], \label{eq:cs:mpc:obj} \\
    \text{s.t.}\;\;\;\; & \; z_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:z} \\
    & \; y_k = H(\ui{z}{LP}) z_k + h(\ui{z}{LP}) - H(\ui{z}{LP})\ui{z}{LP} + d, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:y} \\
    & \; \Delta u_k = u_k - u_{k-1}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:du} \\
    & \; u_{\min} \leq u_k \leq u_{\max}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:u} \\
    & \; y_{\min} \leq y_k \leq y_{\max}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:ycon} \\
    & \; z_0 = \hat{z}(t), \quad d = \hat{d}(t), \quad u_{-1} = u(t-T_\text{s}), \label{eq:cs:mpc:init}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the linearization point \(\ui{z}{LP}\) is the same as in the target optimization problem~\eqref{eq:cs:target}, so that a single linearization of the output mapping is shared by both problems at every sampling instant. The first-order Taylor expansion provides \(y_k\), which enters the problem only through the output constraints~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:ycon}. In the objective function~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:obj}, the deviation from the target is penalized directly in the lifted space by the weighting matrix \(\ui{Q}{z}\), which we propose to derive as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cs:Qz}
  Q_\text{z} = H(\ui{z}{LP})^\intercal Q_\text{y} H(\ui{z}{LP}).
\end{equation}
In this way, the controller is still tuned through the matrix \(\ui{Q}{y}\), which acts on the physical outputs and is therefore convenient to choose and directly comparable with the tuning of the standard formulation~\eqref{eq:of:mpc}. The matrix \(\ui{Q}{z}\) constructed by~\eqref{eq:cs:Qz} is positive semidefinite with rank at most \(n_y\), and it is regularized by a small multiple of the identity matrix so that the MPC problem remains strictly convex.

This is a significant change from the standard Koopman MPC, where the objective function is defined in the original space. 

\subsection{Closed-loop control structure}

The closed-loop structure follows the one shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:close_loop}, with three modifications: a linearization block is added, the observer is a time-varying Kalman filter, and the two optimization problems are replaced by~\eqref{eq:cs:target} and~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc}. The order in which the blocks are evaluated within one sampling period depends on the chosen linearization point. Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop} summarizes the loop for the linearization at the previous target.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Closed-loop control with nonlinear output mapping with linearization at previous target}
  \label{alg:closed-loop}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Koopman lifting function $g(\cdot)$, initial measurement $y(0)$
    \STATE \textbf{Initialization:} $\hat{z}(0) \leftarrow g(y(0))$, $\hat{d}(0) \leftarrow 0$; solve~\eqref{eq:cs:target} with $\ui{z}{LP} = \hat{z}(0)$ and $\hat{d}(0)$ to obtain $\bar{z}(-T_\text{s})$
    \LOOP
      \STATE \textbf{Measurement:} Obtain $y(t)$ from the plant
      \STATE \textbf{Linearization:} Set $\ui{z}{LP} \leftarrow \bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})$ and compute $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ and $h(\ui{z}{LP})$
      \STATE \textbf{Observer:} Run the time-varying Kalman filter with $y(t)$, $u(t-T_\text{s})$ and $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ to obtain the estimates $\hat{z}(t)$ and $\hat{d}(t)$
      \STATE \textbf{Target estimation:} Solve~\eqref{eq:cs:target} with $\hat{d}(t)$ to obtain $\bar{z}(t)$, $\bar{u}(t)$, $\bar{y}(t)$
      \STATE \textbf{MPC:} Solve~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc} with $\hat{z}(t)$, $\hat{d}(t)$, $\bar{z}(t)$ to obtain the optimal first input $u_0^*$
      \STATE \textbf{Apply input:} Apply $u(t) = u_0^*$ to the plant
    \ENDLOOP
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
where the encoder $g : \mathbb{R}^{n_y} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_z}$ provides the initial lifted-state estimate from the first measurement, and the disturbance estimate is initialized at zero. Because the linearization point is the target of the previous sampling instant, the output mapping is linearized before the observer is run, and the same Jacobian is then used by the observer, by the target optimizer, and by the MPC problem. Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop-state} summarizes the loop for the linearization at the current state estimate.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Closed-loop control with nonlinear output mapping (linearization at current state estimate)}
  \label{alg:closed-loop-state}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Koopman lifting function $g(\cdot)$, initial measurement $y(0)$
    \STATE \textbf{Initialization:} $\hat{z}(0) \leftarrow g(y(0))$, $\hat{d}(0) \leftarrow 0$
    \LOOP
      \STATE \textbf{Measurement:} Obtain $y(t)$ from the plant
      \STATE \textbf{Observer:} Run the time-varying Kalman filter with $y(t)$, $u(t-T_\text{s})$ and $H(\hat{z}(t-T_\text{s}))$ to obtain the estimates $\hat{z}(t)$ and $\hat{d}(t)$
      \STATE \textbf{Linearization:} Set $\ui{z}{LP} \leftarrow \hat{z}(t)$ and compute $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ and $h(\ui{z}{LP})$
      \STATE \textbf{Target estimation:} Solve~\eqref{eq:cs:target} with $\hat{d}(t)$ to obtain $\bar{z}(t)$, $\bar{u}(t)$, $\bar{y}(t)$
      \STATE \textbf{MPC:} Solve~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc} with $\hat{z}(t)$, $\hat{d}(t)$, $\bar{z}(t)$ to obtain the optimal first input $u_0^*$
      \STATE \textbf{Apply input:} Apply $u(t) = u_0^*$ to the plant
    \ENDLOOP
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The two algorithms differ in the linearization point and, as a consequence, in the position of the linearization step within the sampling period. In Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop}, a single Jacobian is evaluated at the target of the previous sampling instant and is shared by the observer, by the target optimizer, and by the MPC problem. In Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop-state}, the target optimizer and the MPC problem use the Jacobian at the current state estimate, which becomes available only after the measurement update. The observer itself cannot use this Jacobian, since it is the quantity that the update produces, and its measurement equation is therefore linearized at the estimate of the previous sampling instant.

\subsection{State estimation with a time-varying Kalman filter}
\label{sec:tvkf}

The observer estimates the augmented state $\tilde{z}(t) = [z(t)^\intercal,\; d(t)^\intercal]^\intercal$ from the
noisy output $y(t)$ and the applied input $u(t)$.
The augmented linear dynamics are identical to~\eqref{eq:of:ss} with the original state replaced by the
lifted Koopman state, resulting in
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tvkf:aug}
  \tilde{z}(t+T_\text{s}) = \tilde{A}\,\tilde{z}(t) + \tilde{B}\,u(t),
  \qquad
  \tilde{A} =
  \begin{bmatrix} A & B_d \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix},
  \quad
  \tilde{B} =
  \begin{bmatrix} B \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{z}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z + n_d}$ is the augmented state stacking the lifted Koopman state
and the output disturbance, and $\tilde{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{(n_z+n_d)\times(n_z+n_d)}$ and $\tilde{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{(n_z+n_d)\times n_u}$ are the augmented system matrices.

Because the output map is nonlinear, the measurement equation is re-linearized at each
sampling instant around the linearization point $\ui{z}{LP}$ selected by Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop} or Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop-state}, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tvkf:meas}
  y(t) \approx \tilde{C}(t)\,\tilde{z}(t) + c(t),
  \qquad
  \tilde{C}(t) = \bigl[H(\ui{z}{LP}),\; C_d\bigr],
  \quad
  c(t) = h(\ui{z}{LP}) - H(\ui{z}{LP})\,\ui{z}{LP},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{C}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times (n_z + n_d)}$ is the time-varying output matrix formed by
stacking the Jacobian of $h$ at the linearization point
and the disturbance output matrix $C_d$, and $c(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ is the linearization offset.

With the linear prediction model~\eqref{eq:tvkf:aug} and the time-varying output
matrix~\eqref{eq:tvkf:meas}, the filter runs the standard predict--update recursion at each
sampling instant. The prediction step reads
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tvkf:predict}
  \begin{align}
    \hat{\tilde{z}}^{-}(t) &= \tilde{A}\,\hat{\tilde{z}}(t-T_\text{s}) + \tilde{B}\,u(t-T_\text{s}), \label{eq:tvkf:predict:z} \\
    P^{-}(t) &= \tilde{A}\,P(t-T_\text{s})\,\tilde{A}^\intercal + Q, \label{eq:tvkf:predict:P}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\hat{\tilde{z}}^{-}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z+n_d}$ is the prior augmented-state estimate,
$P^{-}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{(n_z+n_d)\times(n_z+n_d)}$ is the prior error covariance, and
$Q \succeq 0$ is the process noise covariance matrix. The superscript minus sign distinguishes the quantities available before the measurement $y(t)$ has been processed. The update step is then given by
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tvkf:update}
  \begin{align}
    S(t) &= \tilde{C}(t)\,P^{-}(t)\,\tilde{C}(t)^\intercal + R, \label{eq:tvkf:update:S} \\
    K(t) &= P^{-}(t)\,\tilde{C}(t)^\intercal\,S(t)^{-1}, \label{eq:tvkf:update:K} \\
    \hat{\tilde{z}}(t) &= \hat{\tilde{z}}^{-}(t) + K(t)\!\left(y(t) - \tilde{C}(t)\,\hat{\tilde{z}}^{-}(t) - c(t)\right), \label{eq:tvkf:update:z} \\
    P(t) &= \left(I - K(t)\,\tilde{C}(t)\right)P^{-}(t), \label{eq:tvkf:update:P}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $S(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_y}$ is the innovation covariance, $K(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{(n_z+n_d)\times n_y}$ is the Kalman gain, $\hat{\tilde{z}}(t)$ is the posterior augmented-state estimate whose upper $n_z$ entries give $\hat{z}(t)$ and lower $n_d$ entries give $\hat{d}(t)$, $P(t)$ is the posterior error covariance, and $R \succ 0$ is the measurement noise covariance matrix.

\subsection{Block-diagonal deep Koopman transformation}
\label{sec:blockdiag}
During our work, we encountered several problems with suboptimal or infeasible solutions of the Koopman MPC problem solved by the Gurobi solver. To improve the behavior of the optimization, we propose to bring the matrix \(A\) into a block-diagonal structure. The transformation is applied after the Koopman operator has been identified, and it amounts to finding a transformation matrix \(V\) such that
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bd:A}
  \uis{A}{bd} = V^{-1} A V,
\end{equation}
where \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z \times n_z}\) is constructed from the eigenvectors of \(A\) and \(\uis{A}{bd}\) is the real block-diagonal form of \(A\), in which the eigenvalues are grouped into one-by-one blocks for the real eigenvalues and two-by-two blocks for the complex conjugate pairs. The remaining parts of the model have to be modified accordingly, and the same matrix \(V\) is used to transform the input and output matrices as well as the lifted state, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bd:BCz}
  \uis{B}{bd} = V^{-1} B, \quad \uis{C}{bd} = C V, \quad \uis{z}{bd}_k = V^{-1} z_k.
\end{equation}
The Jacobian of the output mapping is transformed in the same way, resulting in
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bd:H}
  \uis{H}{bd}(z_k) = H(z_k) V = H(V \uis{z}{bd}_k) V.
\end{equation}
For a fair comparison, this transformation is applied to every model identified in this work, not only to the Koopman models. The effects of the transformation are discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}.

\section{Identification}
\label{sec:ident}

{\generatedstyle
In this section, we introduce the benchmark process, the data collected from it, and the three prediction models whose closed-loop performance is compared in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}. All models are identified from the same data set with the same scaling, and the parameters reported here are the final ones used in every closed-loop experiment of this work.

\subsection{Reactor--separator process}

The considered plant consists of two continuous stirred tank reactors in series followed by a flash separator, with a recycle stream from the separator back to the first reactor. The plant structure is the one of~\cite{liu2009distributed} and the kinetic, thermodynamic, and geometric parameters are those reported by~\cite{li2019dynamic}. Two consecutive first-order reactions $\text{A} \rightarrow \text{B} \rightarrow \text{C}$ take place in both reactors, where B is the desired product and C an undesired by-product. The plant is therefore a selectivity problem, since additional heat increases the conversion of the reactant A but also the loss of the product B into C.

The plant has nine states, namely the mass fractions of A and B and the temperature in each of the three vessels, and the third vessel is the separator, in which no reaction occurs. The manipulated variables are the three heat duties $Q_1$, $Q_2$, $Q_3$, the fresh feed flow rates $F_{10}$ and $F_{20}$ to the first and the second reactor, and the recycle flow rate $\ui{F}{r}$. The measured and controlled outputs are the three vessel temperatures $T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$ and the bottoms quality $x_{\text{B}3}$, which is the mass fraction of B leaving the separator. The vessel inventories are constant, so the internal flow rates follow from the manipulated ones. The nominal operating point and the box within which the inputs are moved during identification are listed in Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs}.

Two mechanisms make the plant nonlinear. The first one is the Arrhenius dependence of both reaction rates on the vessel temperatures, and the second one is the relative-volatility flash of the separator, which makes the composition of the recycle stream a nonlinear function of the composition in the separator and closes a nonlinear loop back to the first reactor. As a consequence, the bottoms quality is not monotone in the supplied heat: the yield of B reaches a peak and then decreases again as more of the product is converted into C. A model identified around a single operating point can therefore reproduce only one branch of this behavior, which is the reason for choosing this plant as the benchmark. The plant equations are integrated with the explicit Runge--Kutta solver of SciPy, and the sampling period used for identification and for control is $\Ts = 1\,\text{s}$.

\subsection{Identification data}

The excitation is a sequence of $100$ piecewise-constant input steps, each held for $180\,\text{s}$, which gives $18\,000$ samples of input--output data. All six inputs are stepped within the box of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs}, and the levels of the heat duties are paired with the fresh feed flow rate so that the excitation crosses the yield peak of the bottoms quality instead of staying on one of its branches. Over the whole data set the temperatures cover the range from $325\,\text{K}$ to $393\,\text{K}$ and the bottoms quality the range from $0.42$ to $0.77$.

Zero-mean Gaussian measurement noise is added to all four outputs, with a standard deviation equal to $1\%$ of the range of the respective output, that is $0.494\,\text{K}$, $0.508\,\text{K}$, and $0.556\,\text{K}$ for the three temperatures and $0.0033$ for the bottoms quality. The data set is split into the first $12\,960$ samples used for training, the following $2880$ samples used for validation and early stopping, and the remaining $2160$ samples, which are never seen during identification and serve as the test data. Inputs and outputs are standardized to zero mean and unit variance using the statistics of the training split only, and all three models are identified in these coordinates, so that the four outputs contribute to the identification criteria on a comparable scale.

\subsection{Subspace model}

The baseline model is identified with the N4SID algorithm~\cite{van2012subspace} as implemented in the SIPPY package~\cite{armenise2018sippy}, with the direct input--output term fixed to zero. The final model has the order $17$ and the past and future horizons equal to $57$, no centering is applied to the data, and no stability constraint is imposed during identification, which results in the spectral radius of $0.9897$. Identifying this model takes $2.6\,\text{s}$ on a Mac mini with an Apple M4 Pro processor.

\subsection{Koopman models}

Two Koopman models are trained with the loss~\eqref{eq:dk:loss} on the same data. The first one restricts the decoder to a single matrix and thus provides the LTI model required by the standard offset-free Koopman MPC of Sec.~\ref{sec:easy-mpc}. Its lifted dimension is $n_z = 19$ and its encoder is a network with one hidden layer of four GELU units, which gives $666$ trainable parameters in total and the spectral radius of $0.9337$. The second one keeps the nonlinear decoder and is the model used by the proposed scheme. Its lifted dimension is $n_z = 26$, the encoder has the widths $4$--$7$--$14$--$26$ and the decoder the mirrored widths $26$--$14$--$7$--$4$, both with ELU activations, which gives $1884$ trainable parameters and the spectral radius of $0.9622$.

Both models are trained with the Adam optimizer in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} through the Neuromancer library~\cite{Neuromancer2023}, on minibatches of $40$ windows of $N_\text{r} = 120$ samples, for at most $4000$ epochs, with early stopping after $300$ epochs without an improvement of the validation loss and a warm-up of $100$ epochs. The learning rate is $9.9 \cdot 10^{-4}$ for the model with the linear decoder and $8.0 \cdot 10^{-4}$ for the model with the nonlinear decoder, and the loss weights $(\ui{w}{y}, \ui{w}{z}, \ui{w}{r})$ of~\eqref{eq:dk:loss} are $(1.14,\, 2.15,\, 4.84)$ and $(17.38,\, 1.59,\, 2.28)$, respectively. Training took $131\,\text{s}$, terminated by early stopping after $2937$ epochs, for the model with the linear decoder, and $239\,\text{s}$ for the model with the nonlinear decoder, on the same Mac mini with an Apple M4 Pro processor. As stated in Sec.~\ref{sec:blockdiag}, all three models are transformed into the real block-diagonal form before they are used in any control problem.

\subsection{Open-loop accuracy on the test data}

\section{Control setup}
\label{sec:control-setup}

{\generatedstyle
In this section, we describe the closed-loop scenario on which the models of Sec.~\ref{sec:ident} are compared, together with the tuning, the observer, and the benchmark against which every controller is measured. The scenario, the weights, the constraints, and the disturbance sequence are identical in all runs reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}, so that the differences between the controllers are attributable to the prediction model and to the way the output mapping enters the control problem, and not to the way the loop was set up.

All four measured outputs $T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$, and $x_{\text{B}3}$ are controlled, and all six manipulated variables of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs} are available to the controller, so the plant has two more inputs than outputs and the remaining degrees of freedom have to be resolved by the input target of the target optimization. The sampling period is the one used for identification, $\Ts = 1\,\text{s}$, and the prediction horizon is $N = 60$ samples. The input constraints~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:u} are the identification box of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs}, so that the controller never drives the plant outside the region from which the models were identified. The output constraints~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:ycon} are an operating window rather than equipment limits, namely $310\,\text{K} \leq T_i \leq 380\,\text{K}$ for the three vessel temperatures and $0.35 \leq x_{\text{B}3} \leq 0.85$ for the bottoms quality. All signals, weights, and bounds are expressed in the standardized coordinates of Sec.~\ref{sec:ident}, so that the three temperatures and the mass fraction contribute to the objective function on a comparable scale.

\subsection{Grade campaign and reference changes}

The scenario is a grade campaign on the bottoms quality, which is the specification that the separator has to deliver. The plant starts at the nominal steady state of~\cite{li2019dynamic} and the setpoint is held there for the first $350\,\text{s}$, so that the observer of every controller converges from its initialization before the first reference change. At $t = 350\,\text{s}$ the campaign switches to a premium grade, in which the mass fraction of the product B leaving the separator rises from $0.637$ to $0.702$, and at $t = 1000\,\text{s}$ the plant returns to the nominal grade. The whole simulation lasts $1500\,\text{s}$.

Both grades are steady states of the plant, obtained by simulating it to steady state at the nominal input of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs} and at the premium input $[8.0,\, 8.0,\, 8.0,\, 11.0,\, 0.8,\, 3.0]$, and both are reachable inside the identification box, so that no reference change asks for an operating point that the models were never shown. The setpoints of the three temperatures are the corresponding steady-state values, $336.7$, $344.1$, and $346.2\,\text{K}$ at the nominal grade and $331.5$, $338.6$, and $340.1\,\text{K}$ at the premium grade, which means that the controller has to move the whole operating point and not the quality alone. The premium grade is reached with less heat and a larger fresh feed, because the yield of B is not monotone in the supplied heat, and the three temperatures consequently fall by approximately $6\,\text{K}$ instead of rising. This is the direction in which a model identified around a single operating point is most likely to fail, and the grade change is chosen for exactly this reason.

\subsection{Unmeasured feed disturbances}

Two unmeasured feed upsets are applied to the plant while the premium grade is held. At $t = 600\,\text{s}$ the temperature of the fresh feed drops from $313\,\text{K}$ to $310\,\text{K}$, which represents a drift of the feed preheater or of the utility that supplies it. At $t = 850\,\text{s}$ the fresh feed becomes leaner, with the mass fraction of the reactant A falling from $1.00$ to $0.97$, which represents a switch of the feed tank or a carry-over of an inert. Both upsets act on the plant only, and the encoder, the observer, the target optimizer, and the MPC problem are left unchanged, so that the four controllers built on an identified model can reject them only through the output disturbance estimate $\hat{d}(t)$.

The magnitudes are deliberately modest, because both grades have to remain reachable inside the identification box after the upsets. A drop of $10\,\text{K}$ in the feed temperature at the premium throughput would require approximately $40\,\text{kJ/s}$ of additional heat in the first vessel, which exceeds the $25\,\text{kJ/s}$ available there, and would leave the requested grade unreachable for every controller including the benchmark. The measurements are noise-free in all closed-loop runs, although the models were identified from noisy data as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:ident}. This is a deliberate choice, because the differences between the compared controllers are of the same order as the spread that a single noise realization introduces, and reporting one realization would attribute to the formulation what belongs to the noise.

\subsection{Tuning}

The target optimization~\eqref{eq:cs:target} uses $\ui{Q}{y}^\text{t} = \diag([1,\, 1,\, 1,\, 20])$, so that the steady-state target is the reachable operating point closest to the requested grade, with the bottoms quality weighted an order of magnitude above the temperatures. The MPC problem~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc} is tuned in the original setup by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cl:weights}
  \begin{gathered}
    \ui{Q}{y} = \diag([2,\, 2,\, 2,\, 15]), \quad
    Q_{\Delta\text{u}} = \diag([0.5,\, 0.5,\, 0.5,\, 1,\, 1,\, 1]), \\
    \ui{Q}{u} = \diag([0.2,\, 0.2,\, 0.2,\, 0.5,\, 0.5,\, 0.5]),
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where the entries of $\ui{Q}{y}$ follow the order $T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$, $x_{\text{B}3}$ of the outputs and the entries of $Q_{\Delta\text{u}}$ and $\ui{Q}{u}$ the order $Q_1$, $Q_2$, $Q_3$, $F_{10}$, $F_{20}$, $\ui{F}{r}$ of the inputs. The bottoms quality again carries the largest output weight, because it is the variable that the campaign has to meet, while the temperatures are regulated but act mainly as operating limits. The input target towards which $\ui{Q}{u}$ pulls is the nominal utility and throughput setting of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs}, held constant over the whole campaign, so that this term expresses the economic preference of the plant rather than a tracking requirement.

Three tunings are reported. The first one is the original setup~\eqref{eq:cl:weights}. In the second one, $\ui{Q}{y}$ is multiplied by two and everything else is left unchanged, and in the third one, $\ui{Q}{y}$ is divided by two and everything else is left unchanged, so that the relative weight of the outputs against the inputs is moved by a factor of two in each direction. The purpose of this sweep is not to search for a good controller but to test the mechanism of the proposed formulation. The proposed scheme differs from the standard one only in the way the output mapping is used, in the objective function through~\eqref{eq:cs:Qz} and in the output constraints through~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:y}. Its advantage is therefore expected to grow with the weight that the objective function places on the outputs, and to shrink when $\ui{Q}{y}$ is reduced and the outputs become a secondary concern.

\subsection{Observers and the benchmark}

Each controller is paired with the observer that its formulation requires. The two controllers built on an LTI model, namely the one using the subspace model and the one using the Koopman model with the linear decoder, use a stationary Kalman filter on the augmented system~\eqref{eq:of:ss}, while the proposed controllers use the time-varying filter of Sec.~\ref{sec:tvkf}. The number of estimated disturbances equals the number of measurements, $n_d = n_y = 4$, with $B_d = 0$ and $C_d = I$, so that the disturbance is purely additive on the outputs and the rank condition~\eqref{eq:of:rank} is satisfied. The covariances in the standardized coordinates are $P(0) = I$, $R = 0.3\,I$, and $Q = \diag([0.1\,I,\, 0.1\,I])$, with the two blocks acting on the lifted state and on the disturbance, respectively. The only exception is the subspace baseline, whose disturbance block had to be raised to $1.0\,I$ to obtain an acceptable closed-loop response; a larger disturbance covariance produces a faster estimate of $\hat{d}(t)$, so this exception favors the baseline.

The benchmark is a nonlinear MPC that uses the plant equations themselves as the prediction model. It is paired with an extended Kalman filter and with a nonlinear steady-state target selector built on the same equations, both discretized by a fourth-order Runge--Kutta map with ten substeps per sampling period, so that the observer of the benchmark is not biased with respect to the plant stepper. Furthermore, the benchmark is informed of the new feed conditions at the instant at which each upset is applied, so that it suffers neither from process-model mismatch nor from a lag in the estimation of the disturbance. It uses the same horizon, the same weights, the same constraints, and the same references as every other controller. Its closed-loop performance is therefore the practical bound of this comparison, and we use it as the normalization of the results rather than as a competitor.

To evaluate the closed-loop performance, we accumulate over the whole simulation the same quantities that the MPC problem penalizes, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cl:obj}
  J = \underbrace{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \bigl\| y(t) - \ui{y}{sp}(t) \bigr\|_{\ui{Q}{y}}^2}_{\ui{J}{y}}
  + \underbrace{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \bigl\| u(t) - u(t - \Ts) \bigr\|_{Q_{\Delta\text{u}}}^2}_{J_{\Delta\text{u}}}
  + \underbrace{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \bigl\| u(t) - \bar{u}(t) \bigr\|_{\ui{Q}{u}}^2}_{\ui{J}{u}},
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the closed-loop objective, $\ui{J}{y}$, $J_{\Delta\text{u}}$, and $\ui{J}{u}$ are its three parts, $\mathcal{T} = \{0,\, \Ts,\, \ldots,\, (\ui{n}{sim}-1)\Ts\}$ is the set of the sampling instants of the campaign, $\ui{n}{sim} = 1500$ is its length in samples, and $\|v\|_Q^2 = v^\intercal Q v$. The three parts are the closed-loop counterparts of the three terms of~\eqref{eq:of:mpc:obj} and are evaluated with the weights of the tuning that is being reported. A large $\ui{J}{y}$ means that the plant spends time away from the requested grade, which in a campaign is off-specification product, whereas a large $J_{\Delta\text{u}}$ or $\ui{J}{u}$ means that the duties and the flow rates are moved far and held far from the economically preferred operating point, which is paid for in utilities and in throughput.

The target optimization~\eqref{eq:cs:target} and the MPC problem~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc} are quadratic programs, formulated in CVXPY and solved with Gurobi~\cite{gurobi} restricted to a single thread. The decoder $h(\ui{z}{LP})$ and its Jacobian $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ are evaluated in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} at every sampling instant. The nonlinear benchmark and its target selector are formulated in CasADi through the do-mpc toolbox and solved with IPOPT using the MUMPS linear solver, and the plant itself is integrated by the explicit Runge--Kutta solver of SciPy. All closed-loop runs were carried out on a Mac mini with an Apple M4 Pro processor. \todo{add bibliography entries for CVXPY, CasADi, do-mpc, IPOPT, and SciPy}

The second option how to obtain the block diagonal structure is to learn the Koopman operator directly in the block diagonal form. This can be done by limiting the number of learnable parameters in a Koopman matrix during training to those that are in the block diagonal form. This also speeds the training as less parameters are learned.

To obtain an optimal performance, which is neccessary for the real time control, we are proposing to use the block diagonal structure of the Koopman operator. There is no neccessity to use the full Koopman operator, as the block diagonal structure is able to capture the same dynamics of the system and favors the optimization performance. The way this structure is obtained, during or after the training, is not important, as long as the Koopman operator is in the block diagonal form.
