\section{Preliminaries and notation}
\label{sec:Preliminaries}

In this section, we present the preliminary concepts used throughout this paper, namely the offset-free model predictive control (MPC) framework and the Koopman operator theory.
 
\subsection{Offset-free MPC}
In this subsection, we present the standard offset-free MPC framework introduced by~\cite{pannocchia2003disturbance}. The framework consists of three parts, namely a state observer, a target optimization, and a dynamic optimization. Together they introduce an integral action into the MPC, which mitigates the process-model mismatch and ensures offset-free control in the steady state. Without this compensation, the closed loop does not reach a reference other than the origin, because the predictions are made with a model that differs from the plant.

The offset-free augmented system can be written in the following state-space form, including the disturbance input matrix $B_d$ and disturbance output matrix $C_d$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:of:ss}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    x_{k+1} \\
    d_{k+1}
  \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    A & B_d \\
    0 & I
  \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    x_k \\
    d_k
  \end{bmatrix}
  +
  \begin{bmatrix}
    B \\
    0
  \end{bmatrix}
  u_k,
  \qquad
  y_k =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    C & C_d
  \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    x_k \\
    d_k
  \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $x_k$ is the original system state, $d_k$ is the estimated constant disturbance, $u_k$ is the control input, $y_k$ is the output, $A$, $B$, and $C$ are the original state-space matrices, $B_d$ and $C_d$ encode how the disturbance enters the state and output equations, and $I$ is the identity matrix.
Here, $n_x$, $n_u$, $n_y$, and $n_d$ denote the state, input, output, and disturbance dimensions, respectively, so that $x_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x}$, $u_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u}$, $y_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$, $d_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_d}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x \times n_x}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x \times n_u}$, $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_x}$, $B_d \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x \times n_d}$, $C_d \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_d}$, and $I \in \mathbb{R}^{n_d \times n_d}$.

For offset-free behavior, the number of disturbances $n_d$ must be less than or equal to the number of measurements $n_y$. Note that offset-free tracking is achieved in at most as many outputs as there are estimated disturbances. For the case $n_d = n_y$, the choice of the matrices $B_d$ and $C_d$ has to satisfy the necessary condition
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:of:rank}
  \operatorname{rank}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    I - A & -B_d \\
    C & C_d
  \end{bmatrix}
  = n_x + n_y.
\end{equation}
Neither the state nor the disturbance is measured, and both are therefore reconstructed by the observer before the two optimization problems are solved at each sampling instant.

\subsection{Koopman operator}
The Koopman operator theory was introduced by~\cite{koopman1931hamiltonian} and popularized by~\cite{mezic2005spectral}. The theory represents a nonlinear system exactly by a linear operator, by lifting its states or measurements into an infinite-dimensional space in which the lifted variables evolve linearly.

{\generatedstyle\renewcommand{\gensource}{opus5}\genopen
Consider the nonlinear discrete-time system with control
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:koopman:sys}
  x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k),
\end{equation}
where $f : \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \times \mathbb{R}^{n_u} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_x}$ is the nonlinear transition map of the system. For such a system the Koopman operator $\mathcal{K}$ is defined on an extended state space that carries the whole sequence of the future inputs~\citep{korda2018linear}, and it advances the lifting functions, also called the observables, by one step along the trajectories of~\eqref{eq:koopman:sys}, which is written as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:koopman:def}
  \left( \mathcal{K} \psi \right)(x_k,\, \mathbf{u}_k) = \psi\!\left( f\!\left(x_k,\, \mathbf{u}_k(0)\right),\; \mathcal{S}\mathbf{u}_k \right), \quad \forall \psi \in \mathcal{F},
\end{equation}
where $\psi : \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \times \ell(\mathcal{U}) \to \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar lifting function, $\mathcal{F}$ is the space of the lifting functions on which $\mathcal{K}$ is defined, $\mathbf{u}_k \in \ell(\mathcal{U})$ is the sequence of the inputs applied from the step $k$ onwards, $\ell(\mathcal{U})$ is the space of such sequences taking values in the input constraint set $\mathcal{U}$, $\mathbf{u}_k(0)$ is the input applied at the step $k$, and $\mathcal{S}$ is the left shift operator defined by $(\mathcal{S}\mathbf{u}_k)(i) = \mathbf{u}_k(i+1)$. The operator $\mathcal{K}$ is linear even though the map $f$ is not, and this linearity is obtained at the price of an infinite-dimensional $\mathcal{F}$ and of a state space that carries the entire input sequence.

Any practical use of~\eqref{eq:koopman:def} therefore restricts $\mathcal{F}$ to the span of $n_z$ lifting functions of the state alone, which are collected in a single vector-valued map, giving
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:koopman:lift}
  z_k = g(x_k) = \left[ \psi_1(x_k),\; \ldots,\; \psi_{n_z}(x_k) \right]^\intercal,
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the vector-valued lifting map that collects the $n_z$ retained lifting functions and $z_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z}$ is the lifted state. Under this restriction, and with the dependence on the input assumed to be affine, the operator $\mathcal{K}$ reduces to a finite-dimensional linear model in the lifted coordinates that is driven by the input through a constant matrix, which is the structure stated in Sec.~\ref{sec:easy-mpc} and the one that keeps the MPC problem a quadratic program. The model is exact only if the span of $\psi_1, \ldots, \psi_{n_z}$ is invariant under $\mathcal{K}$, so the choice of the lifting functions determines its accuracy, which is why they are identified from data in this work rather than picked from a fixed library. The state $x_k$ of the controlled plant is not measured here, and the lifting map is consequently evaluated at the measured output, $z_k = g(y_k)$ with $g : \mathbb{R}^{n_y} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_z}$.
\genclose}


Over the years, many data-driven approximations of the Koopman operator have been proposed, starting with the connection between the dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) and the Koopman operator theory established by~\cite{rowley2009spectral} and extended to the extended DMD (EDMD) by~\cite{williams2015data}. More recently, neural networks have been used to approximate the lifting functions, so that the lifted system evolves linearly. These algorithms were developed not only for autonomous systems but also for systems with control~\citep{kaiser2021data}. The Koopman operator was brought together with the MPC by~\cite{korda2018linear}, and in recent years almost every kind of MPC has been reformulated to accept an approximation of the Koopman operator, mostly represented as a linear time-invariant (LTI) system in the state-space form. The offset-free version is no exception. \cite{chen2022offset} applied the offset-free control to a soft robot, while~\cite{pan2024offset} applied the same methodology to thermal regulation, which is an application closer to the process control.

\section{Offset-free Koopman MPC}
\label{sec:easy-mpc}

In this section, we present the standard offset-free MPC framework that uses the LTI approximation of the Koopman operator as the prediction model. Since the observer is a standard Kalman filter, we discuss mainly the remaining two optimization problems. The LTI approximation keeps the constraints linear and the objective function quadratic, so that the standard MPC formulation applies without modification. The control structure is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:close_loop}.\todo{Spomenut ze toto je baseline as well as state of the art v koopmanovi?}

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/close_loop.pdf}
  \renewcommand{\gensource}{opus5}%
  \caption{ Basic closed-loop control structure of the offset-free MPC. The observer receives the measured output $y$ and the applied input $u$ and delivers the lifted state estimate $\hat{z}$ and the disturbance estimate $\hat{d}$. The target optimization receives $\hat{d}$ together with the setpoint $y_\text{sp}$ and delivers the steady-state target $\bar{y}$, and the MPC block receives $\bar{y}$, $\hat{z}$, and $\hat{d}$ and delivers the input $u$ applied to the plant. }
  \label{fig:close_loop}
\end{figure}

The target optimization problem computes the steady-state targets $\bar{u}, \bar{z}, \bar{y}$ that minimize the difference between the reference $y_\text{ref}$ and the reachable steady-state output $\bar{y}$. The problem is formulated as:

\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:target:opt}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{\bar{u}, \bar{z}, \bar{y}} \quad & \; (\bar{y} - y_\text{ref})^\intercal \ui{Q}{y}^\text{t} (\bar{y} - y_\text{ref}) \label{eq:target:opt:obj}, \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \; \bar{z} = A\bar{z} + B\bar{u} \label{eq:target:opt:z}, \\
    & \; \bar{y} = C\bar{z} + d \label{eq:target:opt:y}, \\
    & \; u_{\min} \leq \bar{u} \leq u_{\max} \label{eq:target:opt:u}, \\
    & \; y_{\min} \leq \bar{y} \leq y_{\max} \label{eq:target:opt:ycon}, \\
    & \; d = \hat{d}(t), \quad y_\text{ref} = y_\text{sp}(t), \label{eq:target:opt:params}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\bar{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u}$, $\bar{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z}$, and $\bar{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ are the steady-state input, lifted state, and output, respectively; $\ui{Q}{y}^\text{t} \succ 0$ is the output weight of the target optimization, which is tuned separately from the weights of the MPC problem; $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z \times n_z}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z \times n_u}$, $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_z}$ are the state-space matrices of the LTI Koopman model; $d = \hat{d}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ is the additive output disturbance estimate provided by the Kalman filter; $y_\text{ref} = y_\text{sp}(t)$ is the output setpoint; and $u_{\min}, u_{\max}$, $y_{\min}, y_{\max}$ are the input and output constraint bounds.

Given the steady-state target $(\bar{u}, \bar{z}, \bar{y})$, the MPC problem is solved at each sampling instant to compute the optimal input sequence over the horizon $N$:

\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:of:mpc}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{u_0, \ldots, u_{N-1}} \; & \; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left[ (y_k - \bar{y})^\intercal Q_\text{y} (y_k - \bar{y}) + \Delta u_k^\intercal Q_{\Delta\text{u}} \Delta u_k + (u_k - \bar{u})^\intercal Q_\text{u} (u_k - \bar{u}) \right], \label{eq:of:mpc:obj} \\
    \text{s.t.} \;\;\;\; & \; z_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:z} \\
    & \; y_k = C z_k + d, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:y} \\
    & \; \Delta u_k = u_k - u_{k-1}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:du} \\
    & \; u_{\min} \leq u_k \leq u_{\max}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:u} \\
    & \; y_{\min} \leq y_k \leq y_{\max}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:of:mpc:ycon} \\
    & \; z_0 = \hat{z}(t), \quad d = \hat{d}(t), \quad u_{-1} = u(t-T_\text{s}), \label{eq:of:mpc:init}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $N$ is the prediction horizon, $k \in \mathbb{N}_0^{N-1} = \{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\}$ is the prediction step index, and $T_\text{s}$ is the sampling period; $Q_\text{y} \succ 0$ is the output tracking weight matrix, $Q_{\Delta\text{u}} \succ 0$ the input increment weight matrix, and $Q_\text{u} \succeq 0$ the weight on the deviation of the input from its steady-state target; $\Delta u_k$ is the input increment at the step $k$; $\hat{z}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z}$ is the lifted state estimate provided by the Kalman filter; and $u_{-1} = u(t - T_\text{s})$ is the input applied at the previous sampling instant, used to compute the first increment $\Delta u_0$. The first term of the objective function~\eqref{eq:of:mpc:obj} penalizes the predicted tracking error with respect to the output target, the second term penalizes the movement of the manipulated variables, and the third term keeps the inputs at the steady-state target computed by~\eqref{eq:target:opt}, which resolves the remaining degrees of freedom if the plant has more inputs than outputs. Following the receding-horizon principle, only the first element $u_0^*$ of the optimal sequence is applied to the plant.



\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

In this section, we present the proposed offset-free Koopman MPC framework\todo{Hybrid KMPC?}. The main drawback of the current Koopman MPC formulations, whether offset-free, robust, or of any other kind, is that the optimization is carried out without fully exploiting the knowledge of the lifted space.
The prediction is made in the lifted space, but the objective function is expressed in the original space, which is recovered from the lifted state by the linear transformation \(y_k = C z_k\). This recovery is nonlinear by nature, \(y_k = h(z_k)\), and it is restricted to a linear map only so that the MPC problem remains a quadratic program. Formulations that do not use this transformation directly usually rely on an equivalent one, for example by augmenting the lifted state with the measurements, in which case the recovery of the measurements from the lifted state is again linear.
\todo{Intro w citation?}

We show that the lifted space can be used directly in the objective function, and we derive the corresponding transformation of the weighting matrix.
The back transformation is thereby eliminated from the objective function of the MPC problem, while it is still required to compute the steady-state target and to impose the output constraints. In both cases, we approximate it by a first-order Taylor expansion.

The proposed approach applies to any Koopman model whose lifting and projection are nonlinear, independently of the concrete identification.
Either the lifting and the projection are given by one invertible function, as in eDMD, or they are two distinct functions represented by neural networks, as in this work.

\subsection{Linearization of the output mapping}
For the control problem, the transformation from the lifted space back to the original space cannot be eliminated completely, because both the steady-state target and the output constraints are expressed in the original variables. We therefore approximate the output mapping by a first-order Taylor expansion, and we consider two linearization points, the target computed at the previous sampling instant and the current state estimate, which gives
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:linearization}
  \begin{align}
    y_k &= h(z_k) \approx h\!\left(\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})\right) + H\!\left(\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})\right)\!\left(z_k - \bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})\right), \label{eq:linearization:target} \\
    y_k &= h(z_k) \approx h\!\left(\hat{z}(t)\right) + H\!\left(\hat{z}(t)\right)\!\left(z_k - \hat{z}(t)\right), \label{eq:linearization:state}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $h : \mathbb{R}^{n_z} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ is the decoder of the identified model, which acts as the nonlinear output mapping; $H(\cdot) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_z}$ is the Jacobian of $h$ evaluated at its argument; and $\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})$ is the steady-state lifted state computed by the target optimizer at the previous sampling instant.

Both expansions are affine in the lifted state, and they differ only in the point at which the decoder and its Jacobian are evaluated. Denoting this point by $\ui{z}{LP}$ and collecting the two terms that do not depend on $z_k$ into a single offset, the output equation reads
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:linearization:affine}
  y_k = H(\ui{z}{LP})\,z_k + c(t),
  \qquad
  c(t) = h(\ui{z}{LP}) - H(\ui{z}{LP})\,\ui{z}{LP},
\end{equation}
where $\ui{z}{LP}$ is the linearization point, equal to $\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})$ in~\eqref{eq:linearization:target} and to $\hat{z}(t)$ in~\eqref{eq:linearization:state}, and $c(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ is the linearization offset. The Jacobian $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ and the offset $c(t)$ are evaluated once per sampling instant and are therefore constant over the whole prediction horizon of dynamic optimization, while the lifted state $z_k$ is the only quantity that varies along it. The output equation thus keeps the structure of the LTI model of Sec.~\ref{sec:easy-mpc}, with the constant output matrix $C$ replaced by the Jacobian at the linearization point and with the offset $c(t)$ added, so that every optimization problem in which~\eqref{eq:linearization:affine} is substituted remains a quadratic program.

The affine form is accurate only in a neighborhood of $\ui{z}{LP}$, and each of the two candidate points of~\eqref{eq:linearization} favors one of the two optimization problems. The target optimization evaluates the output equation at the steady-state lifted state $\bar{z}$, which the target $\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})$ of the previous sampling instant approximates closely, whereas the dynamic optimization evaluates it along a predicted trajectory that starts at the current estimate $\hat{z}(t)$, so that this second point favors the early part of the prediction horizon. The two problems nevertheless have to share a single linearization point, because the objective function of the dynamic optimization tracks the target delivered by the target optimization, and a target computed under one approximation of the output mapping would not be consistent with predictions made under another. Once the plant settles at the requested operating point, the estimate converges to the target and both candidate points coincide with it, so that the two choices are equivalent in the steady state and differ only during the transients. For this reason, both of them are implemented and compared in this work, in the two closed loops summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop} and Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop-state}, and their behavior is examined in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results} and Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Target optimization}

The objective function of the target optimization is the same as in the standard offset-free MPC of Sec.~\ref{sec:easy-mpc}. The difference is in the output equation, where the linear map of~\eqref{eq:target:opt:y} is replaced by the affine form~\eqref{eq:linearization:affine} of the nonlinear output mapping. The proposed target optimization problem is therefore formulated as:

\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:cs:target}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{\bar{u}, \bar{z}, \bar{y}} \quad & \; (\bar{y} - y_\text{ref})^\intercal \ui{Q}{y}^\text{t} (\bar{y} - y_\text{ref}), \label{eq:cs:target:obj} \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \; \bar{z} = A\bar{z} + B\bar{u}, \label{eq:cs:target:z} \\
    & \; \bar{y} = H(\ui{z}{LP})\bar{z} + h(\ui{z}{LP}) - H(\ui{z}{LP})\ui{z}{LP} + d, \label{eq:cs:target:y} \\
    & \; u_{\min} \leq \bar{u} \leq u_{\max}, \label{eq:cs:target:u} \\
    & \; y_{\min} \leq \bar{y} \leq y_{\max}, \label{eq:cs:target:ycon} \\
    & \; d = \hat{d}(t), \quad y_\text{ref} = y_\text{sp}(t), \label{eq:cs:target:params}%  \quad \ui{z}{LP} \in \left\{ \bar{z}(t-T_\text{s}),\; \hat{z}(t) \right\}, 
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the output equation~\eqref{eq:cs:target:y} is the affine form~\eqref{eq:linearization:affine} written out at the linearization point \(\ui{z}{LP}\), which~\eqref{eq:cs:target:params} fixes to either of the two candidates of~\eqref{eq:linearization}. The problem is stated for both of them, since the formulation does not depend on which one is taken, and the same point is then used by the dynamic optimization of Sec.~\ref{sec:method:mpc}. The two candidates differ in the instant at which the point becomes available. The target \(\bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})\) is delivered by this problem at the previous sampling instant and is therefore known before the measurement of the current one is processed, whereas the state estimate \(\hat{z}(t)\) is delivered by the observer of the current sampling instant and is therefore available only after the measurement update. This difference fixes the order in which the blocks of the closed loop are evaluated, as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:method:cl}.


\subsection{Dynamic optimization}
\label{sec:method:mpc}
The dynamic optimization differs from the standard formulation~\eqref{eq:of:mpc} in the objective function and in the output equation, while the prediction model, the constraints on the inputs, and the input increments are kept unchanged.
The proposed MPC problem is formulated as:
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:cs:mpc}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{u_0, \ldots, u_{N-1}} \; & \; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left[ (z_k - \bar{z})^\intercal Q_\text{z} (z_k - \bar{z}) + \Delta u_k^\intercal Q_{\Delta\text{u}} \Delta u_k + (u_k - \bar{u})^\intercal Q_\text{u} (u_k - \bar{u}) \right], \label{eq:cs:mpc:obj} \\
    \text{s.t.}\;\;\;\; & \; z_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:z} \\
    & \; y_k = H(\ui{z}{LP}) z_k + h(\ui{z}{LP}) - H(\ui{z}{LP})\ui{z}{LP} + d, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:y} \\
    & \; \Delta u_k = u_k - u_{k-1}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:du} \\
    & \; u_{\min} \leq u_k \leq u_{\max}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:u} \\
    & \; y_{\min} \leq y_k \leq y_{\max}, \quad k\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N-1}, \label{eq:cs:mpc:ycon} \\
    & \; z_0 = \hat{z}(t), \quad d = \hat{d}(t), \quad u_{-1} = u(t-T_\text{s}), \label{eq:cs:mpc:init}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the linearization point \(\ui{z}{LP}\) is the same as in the target optimization problem~\eqref{eq:cs:target}, so that a single linearization of the output mapping is shared by both problems at every sampling instant. The first-order Taylor expansion provides \(y_k\), which enters the problem only through the output constraints~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:ycon}. 

In the objective function~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:obj}, the deviation from the target is penalized directly in the lifted space by the weighting matrix \(\ui{Q}{z}\), which we propose to obtain from the output tracking term of the standard formulation~\eqref{eq:of:mpc:obj} by substituting the affine form~\eqref{eq:linearization:affine} for both the predicted output \(y_k\) and the output target \(\bar{y}\). The two are evaluated at the same linearization point and carry the same disturbance estimate, so that the offset \(c(t)\) and the disturbance \(d\) cancel in their difference and the tracking term reduces to a quadratic form in the lifted deviation, which gives
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:cs:Qz}
  \begin{align}
    \begin{split}
      y_k - \bar{y}
        &= \bigl( H(\ui{z}{LP}) z_k + c(t) + d \bigr)
         - \bigl( H(\ui{z}{LP}) \bar{z} + c(t) + d \bigr) \\
        &= H(\ui{z}{LP}) \bigl( z_k - \bar{z} \bigr),
    \end{split} \label{eq:cs:Qz:diff} \\
    (y_k - \bar{y})^\intercal \ui{Q}{y} (y_k - \bar{y}) &= (z_k - \bar{z})^\intercal \ui{Q}{z} (z_k - \bar{z}), \label{eq:cs:Qz:quad} \\
    \ui{Q}{z} &= H(\ui{z}{LP})^\intercal \ui{Q}{y} H(\ui{z}{LP}), \label{eq:cs:Qz:def}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the Jacobian is absorbed into the weighting matrix~\eqref{eq:cs:Qz:def}, which leaves the tracking term~\eqref{eq:cs:Qz:quad} entirely in the lifted coordinates and is the matrix that enters the objective function~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:obj}. The derivation holds for either of the two linearization points, since both the prediction and the target are expanded at whichever one is selected.

In this way, the controller is still tuned through the matrix \(\ui{Q}{y}\), which acts on the physical outputs and is therefore far more convenient to choose than a weighting matrix acting directly on the lifted states, whose entries carry no physical meaning. The tuning of the proposed formulation is kept equivalent to the tuning of the standard formulation~\eqref{eq:of:mpc}. The matrix \(\ui{Q}{z}\) constructed by~\eqref{eq:cs:Qz:def} is positive semidefinite with rank at most \(n_y\).

This is a substantial departure from the standard Koopman MPC, in which the objective function is defined in the original space.
If no output constraints are imposed, the predicted output \(y_k\) disappears from the problem altogether, since it enters only through~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:ycon}, and the dynamic optimization is then solved entirely in the lifted space without any approximation of the output mapping.

\subsection{Closed-loop control structure}
\label{sec:method:cl}

The closed-loop structure follows the one shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:close_loop}, with three modifications: (i) a linearization block is added, (ii) the observer is a time-varying Kalman filter, and (iii) the two optimization problems are replaced by~\eqref{eq:cs:target} and~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc}. The order in which the blocks are evaluated within one sampling period depends on the chosen linearization point. Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop} summarizes the loop for the linearization at the previous target.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Closed-loop control with nonlinear output mapping and linearization at the previous target}
  \label{alg:closed-loop}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Koopman lifting function $g(\cdot)$, initial measurement $y(0)$
    \STATE \textbf{Initialization:} $\hat{z}(0) \leftarrow g(y(0))$, $\hat{d}(0) \leftarrow 0$; solve~\eqref{eq:cs:target} with $\ui{z}{LP} = \hat{z}(0)$ and $\hat{d}(0)$ to obtain $\bar{z}(-T_\text{s})$
    \LOOP
      \STATE \textbf{Measurement:} Obtain $y(t)$ from the plant
      \STATE \textbf{Linearization:} Set $\ui{z}{LP} \leftarrow \bar{z}(t-T_\text{s})$ and compute $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ and $h(\ui{z}{LP})$
      \STATE \textbf{Observer:} Run the time-varying Kalman filter with $y(t)$, $u(t-T_\text{s})$ and $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ to obtain the estimates $\hat{z}(t)$ and $\hat{d}(t)$
      \STATE \textbf{Target estimation:} Solve~\eqref{eq:cs:target} with $\hat{d}(t)$ to obtain $\bar{z}(t)$, $\bar{u}(t)$, $\bar{y}(t)$
      \STATE \textbf{MPC:} Solve~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc} with $\hat{z}(t)$, $\hat{d}(t)$, $\bar{z}(t)$ to obtain the optimal first input $u_0^*$
      \STATE \textbf{Apply input:} Apply $u(t) = u_0^*$ to the plant
    \ENDLOOP
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
In Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop}, the encoder $g$ provides the initial lifted-state estimate from the first measurement, and the disturbance estimate is initialized at zero. Because the linearization point is the target of the previous sampling instant, the output mapping is linearized before the observer is run, and the same Jacobian is then used by the observer, by the target optimizer, and by the MPC problem. Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop-state} summarizes the loop for the linearization at the current state estimate.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Closed-loop control with nonlinear output mapping and linearization at the current state estimate}
  \label{alg:closed-loop-state}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Koopman lifting function $g(\cdot)$, initial measurement $y(0)$
    \STATE \textbf{Initialization:} $\hat{z}(0) \leftarrow g(y(0))$, $\hat{d}(0) \leftarrow 0$
    \LOOP
      \STATE \textbf{Measurement:} Obtain $y(t)$ from the plant
      \STATE \textbf{Observer:} Run the time-varying Kalman filter with $y(t)$, $u(t-T_\text{s})$ and $H(\hat{z}(t-T_\text{s}))$ to obtain the estimates $\hat{z}(t)$ and $\hat{d}(t)$
      \STATE \textbf{Linearization:} Set $\ui{z}{LP} \leftarrow \hat{z}(t)$ and compute $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ and $h(\ui{z}{LP})$
      \STATE \textbf{Target estimation:} Solve~\eqref{eq:cs:target} with $\hat{d}(t)$ to obtain $\bar{z}(t)$, $\bar{u}(t)$, $\bar{y}(t)$
      \STATE \textbf{MPC:} Solve~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc} with $\hat{z}(t)$, $\hat{d}(t)$, $\bar{z}(t)$ to obtain the optimal first input $u_0^*$
      \STATE \textbf{Apply input:} Apply $u(t) = u_0^*$ to the plant
    \ENDLOOP
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The two algorithms differ in the linearization point and, as a consequence, in the position of the linearization step within the sampling period. In Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop}, a single Jacobian is evaluated at the target of the previous sampling instant and is shared by the observer, by the target optimizer, and by the MPC problem. In Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop-state}, the target optimizer and the MPC problem use the Jacobian at the current state estimate, which becomes available only after the measurement update. The observer itself cannot use this Jacobian, since it is the quantity that the update produces, and its measurement equation is therefore linearized at the estimate of the previous sampling instant.

\subsection{State estimation with a time-varying Kalman filter}
\label{sec:tvkf}

The observer estimates the augmented state $\tilde{z}(t) = [z(t)^\intercal,\; d(t)^\intercal]^\intercal$ from the
noisy output $y(t)$ and the applied input $u(t)$.
The augmented linear dynamics are identical to~\eqref{eq:of:ss} with the original state replaced by the
lifted Koopman state, resulting in
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tvkf:aug}
  \tilde{z}(t+T_\text{s}) = \tilde{A}\,\tilde{z}(t) + \tilde{B}\,u(t),
  \qquad
  \tilde{A} =
  \begin{bmatrix} A & B_d \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix},
  \quad
  \tilde{B} =
  \begin{bmatrix} B \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{z}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z + n_d}$ is the augmented state stacking the lifted Koopman state
and the output disturbance, and $\tilde{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{(n_z+n_d)\times(n_z+n_d)}$ and $\tilde{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{(n_z+n_d)\times n_u}$ are the augmented system matrices.

Because the output mapping is nonlinear, the measurement equation is re-linearized at each
sampling instant around the linearization point $\ui{z}{LP}$ selected by Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop} or Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop-state}, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tvkf:meas}
  y(t) \approx \tilde{C}(t)\,\tilde{z}(t) + c(t),
  \qquad
  \tilde{C}(t) = \bigl[H(\ui{z}{LP}),\; C_d\bigr],
  \quad
  c(t) = h(\ui{z}{LP}) - H(\ui{z}{LP})\,\ui{z}{LP},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{C}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times (n_z + n_d)}$ is the time-varying output matrix formed by
stacking the Jacobian of $h$ at the linearization point
and the disturbance output matrix $C_d$, and $c(t)$ is the offset of~\eqref{eq:linearization:affine}.

With the linear prediction model~\eqref{eq:tvkf:aug} and the time-varying output
matrix~\eqref{eq:tvkf:meas}, the filter runs the standard predict--update recursion at each
sampling instant. The prediction step reads
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tvkf:predict}
  \begin{align}
    \hat{\tilde{z}}^{-}(t) &= \tilde{A}\,\hat{\tilde{z}}(t-T_\text{s}) + \tilde{B}\,u(t-T_\text{s}), \label{eq:tvkf:predict:z} \\
    P^{-}(t) &= \tilde{A}\,P(t-T_\text{s})\,\tilde{A}^\intercal + Q, \label{eq:tvkf:predict:P}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\hat{\tilde{z}}^{-}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z+n_d}$ is the prior augmented-state estimate,
$P^{-}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{(n_z+n_d)\times(n_z+n_d)}$ is the prior error covariance, and
$Q \succeq 0$ is the process noise covariance matrix. The superscript minus sign distinguishes the quantities available before the measurement $y(t)$ has been processed. The update step is then given by
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:tvkf:update}
  \begin{align}
    S(t) &= \tilde{C}(t)\,P^{-}(t)\,\tilde{C}(t)^\intercal + R, \label{eq:tvkf:update:S} \\
    K(t) &= P^{-}(t)\,\tilde{C}(t)^\intercal\,S(t)^{-1}, \label{eq:tvkf:update:K} \\
    \hat{\tilde{z}}(t) &= \hat{\tilde{z}}^{-}(t) + K(t)\!\left(y(t) - \tilde{C}(t)\,\hat{\tilde{z}}^{-}(t) - c(t)\right), \label{eq:tvkf:update:z} \\
    P(t) &= \left(I - K(t)\,\tilde{C}(t)\right)P^{-}(t), \label{eq:tvkf:update:P}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $S(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_y}$ is the innovation covariance, $K(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{(n_z+n_d)\times n_y}$ is the Kalman gain, $\hat{\tilde{z}}(t)$ is the posterior augmented-state estimate whose upper $n_z$ entries give $\hat{z}(t)$ and lower $n_d$ entries give $\hat{d}(t)$, $P(t)$ is the posterior error covariance, and $R \succ 0$ is the measurement noise covariance matrix.

\subsection{Block-diagonal deep Koopman transformation}
\label{sec:blockdiag}
In this work, we encountered suboptimal or infeasible solutions of the Koopman MPC problem when it was solved with the Gurobi solver. To improve the behavior of the optimization, we propose to bring the matrix \(A\) into a block-diagonal structure. The transformation is applied after the Koopman operator has been identified, and it amounts to finding a transformation matrix \(V\) such that
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bd:A}
  \uis{A}{bd} = V^{-1} A V,
\end{equation}
where \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{n_z \times n_z}\) is constructed from the eigenvectors of \(A\) and \(\uis{A}{bd}\) is the real block-diagonal form of \(A\), in which the real eigenvalues form one-by-one blocks and the complex conjugate pairs form two-by-two blocks. The remaining parts of the model have to be modified accordingly, and the same matrix \(V\) is used to transform the input and output matrices as well as the lifted state, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bd:BCz}
  \uis{B}{bd} = V^{-1} B, \quad \uis{C}{bd} = C V, \quad \uis{z}{bd}_k = V^{-1} z_k.
\end{equation}
The Jacobian of the output mapping is transformed in the same way, resulting in
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bd:H}
  \uis{H}{bd}(z_k) = H(z_k) V = H(V \uis{z}{bd}_k) V.
\end{equation}
For a fair comparison, this transformation is applied to every model identified in this work, not only to the Koopman models. The effects of the transformation are discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}.

\section{Identification}
\label{sec:ident}


In this section, we introduce the benchmark process, the data collected from it, and the three prediction models whose closed-loop performance is compared in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}.

\subsection{Reactor--reactor--separator process}

The considered plant consists of two continuous stirred tank reactors in series followed by a flash separator, with a recycle stream from the separator back to the first reactor. {\generatedstyle\renewcommand{\gensource}{opus5.5}\genopen The plant structure follows~\cite{liu2009distributed}, and the kinetic, thermodynamic, and geometric parameters are those reported by~\cite{li2019dynamic}.\genclose} Two consecutive first-order reactions $\text{A} \rightarrow \text{B} \rightarrow \text{C}$ take place in both reactors, where B is the desired product and C an undesired by-product. {\generatedstyle\renewcommand{\gensource}{opus5.5}\genopen The control of the plant is therefore a selectivity problem, since additional heat increases the conversion of the reactant A but also the loss of the product B into C.\genclose}

The plant has nine states, namely the mass fractions of A and B and the temperature in each of the three vessels, and the third vessel is the separator, in which no reaction occurs. The manipulated variables are the three heat duties $Q_1$, $Q_2$, $Q_3$ and the fresh feed flow rate $F_{10}$ to the first reactor. The fresh feed flow rate $F_{20}$ to the second reactor and the recycle flow rate $\ui{F}{r}$ are held fixed at the nominal values $0.5\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}$ and $4.0\,\text{m}^3/\text{s}$. The measured and controlled outputs are the three vessel temperatures $T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$ and the bottoms quality $x_{\text{B}3}$, which is the mass fraction of B leaving the separator. The vessel inventories are constant, so the internal flow rates follow from $F_{10}$ together with two fixed flows. The nominal operating point and the box within which the manipulated inputs are moved during identification are listed in Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs}.

Two mechanisms make the plant nonlinear. The first one is the Arrhenius dependence of both reaction rates on the vessel temperatures, and the second one is the relative-volatility flash of the separator, which makes the composition of the recycle stream a nonlinear function of the composition in the separator and closes a nonlinear loop back to the first reactor. As a consequence, the bottoms quality is not monotone in the supplied heat: the yield of B reaches a peak and then decreases again as more of the product is converted into C. A model identified around a single operating point can therefore reproduce only one branch of this behavior, which is the reason for choosing this plant as the benchmark. The plant equations are integrated in Python with the explicit Runge--Kutta solver of SciPy, and the sampling period used for identification and for control is $\Ts = 1\,\text{s}$.

\subsection{Identification data}

The excitation is a sequence of $100$ piecewise-constant input steps, each held for $180\,\text{s}$, which gives $18\,000$ samples of input--output data. The hold time of $180\,\text{s}$ is long enough for the plant to settle at a new steady state after every step, so that the data contain both the transient and the steady-state response to each input change. All four manipulated inputs are stepped within the box of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs}.
%, and the levels of the heat duties are paired with $F_{10}$ so that the excitation crosses the yield peak of the bottoms quality instead of staying on one of its branches.
Over the whole data set the temperatures cover the range from $326\,\text{K}$ to $396\,\text{K}$ and the bottoms quality the range from $0.40$ to $0.76$.

Zero-mean Gaussian measurement noise is added to all four outputs, with a standard deviation equal to $1\%$ of the range of the respective output, that is $0.487\,\text{K}$, $0.527\,\text{K}$, and $0.610\,\text{K}$ for the three temperatures and $0.0036$ for the bottoms quality.
The data set is split into the first $12\,960$ ($72\%$) samples used for training, the following $2880$ ($16\%$) samples used for validation and early stopping, and the remaining $2160$ ($12\%$) samples, which are never seen during identification and serve as the test data. Inputs and outputs are standardized to zero mean and unit variance using the statistics of the training split only, and all three models are identified in these coordinates, so that the different scales of the temperatures and of the mass fraction do not bias the identification.

\subsection{Subspace model}


The industrial baseline model is identified with numerical algorithms for subspace state-space system identification (N4SID)~\cite{van2012subspace}, as implemented in the SIPPY package~\cite{armenise2018sippy}. The direct input--output term is fixed to zero, and the past and future horizons are both held at $20$ samples. The model of order $6$ attains the smallest mean absolute error (MAE) on the standardized validation split, and this model, whose spectral radius is $0.9865$, is used for the closed-loop comparison.

\subsection{Koopman models}

The two Koopman models share the structure of the encoder and of the linear lifted dynamics, but each model is trained separately with its own hyperparameters, and they differ in the map from the lifted state to the output. The first model restricts this map to a matrix $C$, so the output is a linear projection of the lifted state. It is the LTI model required by the standard offset-free Koopman MPC of Sec.~\ref{sec:easy-mpc}, and it is the model used by the Koopman baseline in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}. 

The second model keeps the nonlinear output map $h(\cdot)$ as proposed in Sec.~\ref{sec:method}, realized by a decoder whose hidden layer mirrors the encoder. This model is used by both variants of the proposed formulation, Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop} and Algorithm~\ref{alg:closed-loop-state}. The two variants share the network, the weights, and the block-diagonal transformation of Sec.~\ref{sec:blockdiag}, and they differ only in the point at which $h(\cdot)$ is linearized. Any difference between them in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results} is therefore attributable to that linearization point.

The hyperparameters of each Koopman model are selected by a separate search in Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna}, and both networks are trained with the Adam optimizer in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} through the Neuromancer library~\cite{Neuromancer2023}. \section{Control setup}
\label{sec:control-setup}

In this section, we describe the closed-loop scenario on which the models of Sec.~\ref{sec:ident} are compared, together with the tuning, the observer, and the benchmark against which every controller is measured. The scenario, the weights, the constraints, and the disturbance sequence are identical in all runs reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:Results}, so that the differences between the controllers are attributable to the prediction model and to the way the output mapping enters the control problem, and not to the way the loop was set up.

All four measured outputs $T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$, and $x_{\text{B}3}$ are controlled, and the four manipulated variables of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs} are available to the controller, so the plant is square and the output specifications determine the steady-state target. The sampling period is the one used for identification, $\Ts = 1\,\text{s}$, and the prediction horizon is $N = 60$ samples. The input constraints~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:u} are the identification box of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs}. The output constraints~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc:ycon} are an operating window rather than equipment limits, namely $310\,\text{K} \leq T_i \leq 380\,\text{K}$ for the three vessel temperatures and $0.35 \leq x_{\text{B}3} \leq 0.85$ for the bottoms quality\todo{actually neccessary? not hitting, also make table not in text?}. \subsection{Tuning}

The target optimization~\eqref{eq:cs:target} uses $\ui{Q}{y}^\text{t} = \diag([1,\, 1,\, 1,\, 20])$, so that the steady-state target is the reachable operating point closest to the requested grade, with the bottoms quality weighted an order of magnitude above the temperatures. The MPC problem~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc} is tuned in the original setup by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cl:weights}
  \begin{gathered}
    \ui{Q}{y} = \diag([2,\, 2,\, 2,\, 15]), \quad
    Q_{\Delta\text{u}} = \diag([0.5,\, 0.5,\, 0.5,\, 1]), \\
    \ui{Q}{u} = \diag([0.2,\, 0.2,\, 0.2,\, 0.5]),
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where the entries of $\ui{Q}{y}$ follow the order $T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$, $x_{\text{B}3}$ of the outputs and the entries of $Q_{\Delta\text{u}}$ and $\ui{Q}{u}$ the order $Q_1$, $Q_2$, $Q_3$, $F_{10}$ of the inputs. The bottoms quality again carries the largest output weight, because it is the variable that the campaign has to meet, while the temperatures are regulated but act mainly as operating limits. The input target towards which $\ui{Q}{u}$ pulls is the nominal utility and throughput setting of Table~\ref{tab:cstr-sep-inputs}, held constant over the whole campaign, so that this term expresses the economic preference of the plant rather than a tracking requirement.

\subsection{Observers}

Each controller is paired with the observer that its formulation requires. The two controllers built on an LTI model, namely the one using the subspace model and the one using the Koopman model with the linear decoder, use a stationary Kalman filter on the augmented system~\eqref{eq:of:ss}, while the proposed controllers use the time-varying filter of Sec.~\ref{sec:tvkf}. The number of estimated disturbances equals the number of measurements, $n_d = n_y = 4$, with $B_d = 0$ and $C_d = I$, so that the disturbance is purely additive on the outputs and the rank condition~\eqref{eq:of:rank} is satisfied. The covariances in the standardized coordinates are $P(0) = I$, $R = 0.3\,I$, and $Q = \diag([0.1\,I,\, 0.1\,I])$, with the two blocks acting on the lifted state and on the disturbance, respectively.

% The benchmark is a nonlinear MPC that uses the plant equations themselves as the prediction model. It is paired with an extended Kalman filter and with a nonlinear steady-state target selector built on the same equations, both discretized by a fourth-order Runge--Kutta map with ten substeps per sampling period, so that the observer of the benchmark is not biased with respect to the plant stepper. Furthermore, the benchmark is informed of the new feed conditions at the instant at which each upset is applied, so that it suffers neither from process-model mismatch nor from a lag in the estimation of the disturbance. It uses the same horizon, the same weights, the same constraints, and the same references as every other controller. Its closed-loop performance is therefore the practical bound of this comparison, and we use it as the normalization of the results rather than as a competitor.
\subsection{Evaluation}

To evaluate the closed-loop performance, we accumulate over the whole simulation the same quantities that the MPC problem penalizes, which gives
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:cl:obj}
  \begin{align}
    \ui{J}{y} &= \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \bigl\| y(t) - \ui{y}{sp}(t) \bigr\|_{\ui{Q}{y}}^2, \label{eq:cl:obj:y} \\
    J_{\Delta\text{u}} &= \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \bigl\| u(t) - u(t - \Ts) \bigr\|_{Q_{\Delta\text{u}}}^2, \label{eq:cl:obj:du} \\
    \ui{J}{u} &= \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \bigl\| u(t) - \bar{u}(t) \bigr\|_{\ui{Q}{u}}^2, \label{eq:cl:obj:u} \\
    J &= \ui{J}{y} + J_{\Delta\text{u}} + \ui{J}{u}, \label{eq:cl:obj:sum}
  \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\ui{J}{y}$ is the closed-loop output cost, $\|v\|_Q^2 = v^\intercal Q v$, and $\mathcal{T} = \{0,\, \Ts,\, \ldots,\, (\ui{n}{sim}-1)\Ts\}$ is the set of the sampling instants of the campaign, with $\ui{n}{sim} = 1500$ its length in samples; $J_{\Delta\text{u}}$ is the closed-loop input-increment cost and $\ui{J}{u}$ the closed-loop input-target cost; and $J$ is their sum. The three parts are the closed-loop counterparts of the three terms of~\eqref{eq:of:mpc:obj} and are evaluated with the weights of the tuning that is being reported. 
%A large $\ui{J}{y}$ means that the plant spends time away from the requested grade, which in a campaign is off-specification product, whereas a large $J_{\Delta\text{u}}$ or $\ui{J}{u}$ means that the duties and the flow rates are moved far and held far from the economically preferred operating point, which is paid for in utilities and in throughput.

The target optimization~\eqref{eq:cs:target} and the MPC problem~\eqref{eq:cs:mpc} are quadratic programs, formulated in CVXPY and solved with Gurobi~\cite{gurobi}. The decoder $h(\ui{z}{LP})$ and its Jacobian $H(\ui{z}{LP})$ are evaluated in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} at every sampling instant. 
%The nonlinear benchmark and its target selector are formulated in CasADi through the do-mpc toolbox and solved with IPOPT using the MUMPS linear solver, and the plant itself is integrated by the explicit Runge--Kutta solver of SciPy. 
All closed-loop runs were carried out on a Mac mini with an Apple M4 Pro processor. \todo{add bibliography entries for CVXPY}

The second option how to obtain the block diagonal structure is to learn the Koopman operator directly in the block diagonal form. This can be done by limiting the number of learnable parameters in a Koopman matrix during training to those that are in the block diagonal form. This also speeds the training as less parameters are learned.

To obtain an optimal performance, which is neccessary for the real time control, we are proposing to use the block diagonal structure of the Koopman operator. There is no neccessity to use the full Koopman operator, as the block diagonal structure is able to capture the same dynamics of the system and favors the optimization performance. The way this structure is obtained, during or after the training, is not important, as long as the Koopman operator is in the block diagonal form.
